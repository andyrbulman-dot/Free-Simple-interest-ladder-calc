Interest owed to you — the increasing principal sum

I built this tool to work out exactly how much my property agent owed me after an error led to an ongoing rent increase. Once I had the sums right for myself, I put it online for free to save other tenants the headache of working it out by hand.

How the sums work

Most calculators put interest on one lump sum from one date. That is wrong for rent overpayments, because you paid month by month: the principal sum owed to you was increasing the whole time. Your first overpayment has been held the longest and earns the most interest; your last one the least. This tool adds each payment to the running balance on the day it was made, charges simple interest on that balance for the days until the next payment (balance × rate × days ÷ 365), and adds it all up. Interest is never charged on interest. A late first payment does not shift the rest — later payments are counted on the day the rent falls due.

The 8% rate is the usual county-court rate (County Courts Act 1984, s.69); you can change it on the page if the court directs a different one.

Paying off arrears — the decreasing principal sum

This tool is to help tenants figure out a fair instalment plan when they fall behind on rent. If your landlord or a court applies statutory interest to your debt, you should only pay interest on what is actually left to pay — not the original lump sum. This calculation tracks that shrinking balance monthly payment by monthly payment (or weekly, fortnightly or quarterly if that is how you pay).

How the engine works

Standard debt calculators apply interest to the whole lump sum all at once, which is a scam. As you pay off your arrears payment by payment, the core debt shrinks. This tool calculates flat, non-compounding simple interest (like the UK statutory 8% rate) on that declining balance. Every time you make a payment, the principal drops, and the interest charge goes down with it. The loop runs until the debt hits zero, tracking the exact number of payments, the final odd-sized payment, and the true interest cost.

The legal bit

These tools are provided as they are, with no guarantees. They are a guide, not formal legal advice, and you use them entirely at your own risk. I am not a solicitor and accept no liability for your claim, your debt or your tenancy status.

Double-check every figure before you rely on it. Check it with Citizens Advice or Shelter before putting it to a court, a tribunal, or in a Letter Before Action, and before you put a repayment figure to your landlord — make sure you are not accidentally working from an increased amount and overpaying. If you are relying on the arrears figures as a defence against eviction, make sure they are cast iron first. If a landlord turns down a mathematically correct repayment plan, showing that you tried to establish a reasonable schedule can form part of your legal backing — but only if the numbers are completely accurate.
